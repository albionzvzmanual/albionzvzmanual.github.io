# Defensive Layering

Defensive layering combines tools that answer different parts of a threat. They may cover the same players at the same moment, or protect those players in different places as they move.

[Jump to the defensive layering diagram](#layering-diagram){ .tactical-jump }

## Core idea

Choose what must be covered, what event your tool answers and who can reach it. Keep track of any threat that will still need a response afterward.

## Common defensive layers

- spacing
- personal defensives
- tank stops and interrupts
- Locus / cleanse effects
- Oathkeeper/resistance support
- Rootbound or defensive zone tools
- Judicator / Guard / Demon-style armor tools
- direct healing from the assigned recovery kit
- healing over time and zones where the selected kit provides them

## Danger area vs safe area

The most common failure is defensive value on the wrong place.

Examples:

- Locus on safe backline while the choke dies.
- Oathkeepers too far from the threatened group.
- Nature zone where nobody should stand.
- tank stop after the channel already hits.
- personal defensives after the bomb already finished.

## Timing

An enemy group turning together can be a cue to get into reach. It does not show how many tools they will spend or who will take the hit. Prepare a position from which you can act without sharing the same exposure. Choose the activation cue according to the effect and what you can observe.

## Layering logic

| Assigned purpose | When it can help |
| --- | --- |
| Prevention | Have the appropriate protection active when the expected threat reaches its targets. |
| Disruption | Stop or delay an action while there is still time to change its result. Check whether your effect can interrupt or redirect it. |
| Reactive response | Respond to an observed event, such as allies being caught by an effect your selected tool can remove. |
| Recovery | Reach survivors while healing or cover can still help them regain a usable position. That may be along the return route rather than at impact. |
| Deliberate overlap | Use two effects together if both solve part of a credible threat or one alone is unlikely to provide enough coverage. Check which response will remain available. |

## Practice drill

A small enemy group moves toward the lead players at a crossing. Another group waits at the bend. Before the first group commits, mark where you could act, what you know about each group, and what would trigger your assigned effect.

??? question "The first stop buys enough time for the lead to separate. What remains?"

    Check that the lead can recover and that the bend is still watched. Hold the extra tool if the immediate threat no longer needs it and it is assigned to the next wave.

??? question "The first wave still threatens several trapped allies. What changes?"

    Overlap can be appropriate if each effect helps the trapped group survive. Check whether another player can cover the bend or whether the route must change now that the reserve is spent.

In a replay, mark the observed threat, the coverage position, the intended trigger, when the effect reached the group, and who could still protect the next move. A missed trigger, a tool out of reach, and a deliberate hold call for different feedback. If one view cannot show whether the intended allies received the effect, mark that uncertainty.
